Project 01 · Sooner Racing · Powertrain

Formula SAE Cooling Package

Sized and packaged a temporary radiator, then designed and fabricated its mounts, so the car could run on track a month before the final cooling system existed.

Role · Powertrain subteamScope · Sizing, packaging, fabricationTools · SolidWorks, shop fabrication
The problem

The car needed seat time. The cooling package did not exist yet.

The final cooling system was still in design. Meanwhile the car needed to run: driver training, shakedown, and the data you only get from actually driving the thing. Every week without a radiator was a week the team could not turn the engine over on track.

So the job was never design the best radiator. It was get a radiator on this car that is good enough to test behind, without spending parts, money or chassis real estate that the real package was going to need later.

A temporary part has a requirement a permanent one does not: it has to be easy to take back off.

Sizing

At autocross speeds, frontal area beats thickness.

The limiting case for this car is not top speed. Testing is short runs, low average speed and a lot of idling, which means the radiator spends most of its life without much air being forced through it. When airflow is the scarce thing, a bigger face does more for you than a deeper core, because a deeper core just adds pressure drop to air you are already struggling to move.

That reasoning landed on a roughly 12 by 18 inch core with a hex-cell matrix. The hex core buys surface area per unit of frontal area, and pays for it in air-side pressure drop. That trade is only worth taking if you can actually keep air moving through the face, which turned into a requirement on the mount: leave the core face clear, and do not let air find an easier path around it than through it.

Mounting

Let the frame take the load. The core is not a structural member.

A radiator core is thin-walled brazed material. It is very good at holding coolant and very bad at being a chassis component. The obvious mount, bolting through the core tabs straight into the frame, feeds every bump and every bit of vibration directly into the thing you least want to crack.

So I designed a support frame that carries the load, and captured the core inside it. The brackets tie the frame to existing chassis tubes, the core sits in the frame, and nothing structural passes through the radiator itself. It also made removal a matter of undoing the brackets rather than unpicking the whole install, which matters when the part is explicitly temporary.

01

Mount to what exists

Ties to chassis tubes that were already there. No permanent modification to a frame that a properly designed cooling package still has to use.

02

Square to the airflow

The frame holds the core face perpendicular to the incoming air. A radiator mounted crooked is a radiator with less effective frontal area than you sized for.

03

Survive track loads

Coolant makes the assembly heavy, and a tube-frame car over curbing is not gentle. The support frame is what takes vibration and shock, not the core.

Result

A month of testing the team would not otherwise have had.

The car ran on the temporary package for about a month of on-track testing, and the engine stayed inside its safe operating band through the sessions. The testing schedule held, which was the entire point of the exercise.

In hindsight: I would have instrumented it. A coolant temperature log against ambient and session type would have turned it stayed in range into an actual number, and handed the team real data to size the final package from instead of a pass or fail.
